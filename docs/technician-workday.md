# Technician Workday — Source Reference

The technician's own account of a paving-density workday, in order, cleaned up from a spoken walkthrough. It is raw material for designing the workflow and the data the app records, not a specification. Numbers and habits marked *rule of thumb* are the technician's practice, not universal requirements. Steps often overlap or happen in a different order; this is the usual sequence.

## 1. Open the plant

- Open the garage/parking area; unlock exterior doors, radiation storage rooms and closets; open the main office.
- Turn on the ovens. Turn the burners to low to warm tools. Start warming the sink water (hot tap on low). Turn the office AC up or adjust it.
- Put the PQI gauge on its charger.
- Run the nuclear gauge standard check for asphalt content; record the CHI and standard number.

## 2. Plan with the scale house and the plant lab

Before leaving, check with the scale house:
- the planned mix or mixes, early production, standby status and its reason, whether the plant is running, when production/shipping is expected to start, and any plan changes.
- Plans are expectations, not guarantees.

Then coordinate with the plant lab technician who stays at the plant: share and re-verify what the scale house said, ask about newer information, sort out any differences, and agree how to communicate during the day.

Collect: project number(s), jobsite location, mix identification, and production/testing details. The mix information is needed to set up the PQI.

*Saved and reused*: project, location and mix, carried into the paving, sample, core and test records.

## 3. Load up for the jobsite

Order varies. The point is to find problems at the plant, not at the jobsite.

- **Trailer:** hitch, hitch pin, safety chains, trailer lights.
- **Generator:** check the gasoline, start it and run it to verify.
- **Water:** enough for the coring rig; fill the trailer barrel. Water must be ready before coring.
- **Ice and core storage:** enough ice for the expected cores. Usual setup is two insulated cooler barrels. The amount depends on how many cores are expected and where the ice comes from.
- **Marking and core tools:** lumber crayon, marking paint, the core-removal tool (a welded handle on about half a core drill, used to pry cores out).
- **General tools:** shovel, a container of asphalt material for filling holes, hand tamp if available, boards to level or correct the coring rig's footing.
- **Measuring:** tape measure for core thickness; measuring wheel for stations and offsets.
- **PQI and paperwork:** the charged PQI, the red book primed with the jobsite information, a pen.
- **Truck fuel:** enough for the day. Rule of thumb: fill up when below half a tank. Refuel before the first load leaves if needed.

## 4. Travel to the jobsite

- At an unfamiliar site, often wait for the first truck to load and follow it.
- Get the address or GPS location and the road identity, so the site can be found independently if separated from the truck.
- Confirm fuel, destination, equipment, supplies and water. Water can be filled before leaving or later; the technician may wait by the silos to watch the first load being loaded.

## 5. Arrive and set up at the jobsite

**Locate and park.** Find the active paving area; may drive the site's length to understand the layout. Park safely behind cones, preferably near where the roller starts. Ideally arrive before or as the first load does, to talk with the roller operator and see how the operation runs. Stay clear of paving, traffic and equipment.

**Meet the roller operator.** Ask whether an established rolling pattern exists; get it and record it. If none, decide whether a trial pattern is needed. Mix type, lift thickness and the initial condition of the mat all influence the pattern. Direct, repeated communication may be needed.

**Record the initial paving information.** Starting station, lane and direction (inner/outer), paving rate if available, and width being placed. Best captured before or as paving starts.

**Paving rate and thickness.** Rule of thumb: every 110 pounds of paving rate corresponds roughly to one inch of compacted asphalt. Paving rate and width affect whether the roller can keep up; paver speed or rolling may need adjusting.

**State inspector.** Ask whether core locations are available yet. Get contact and phone information for the right state-side person if possible, in case the inspector can't be found or core delivery and return need coordinating. Keep the contact with the project/jobsite.

**Rolling pattern vocabulary.**
- A *pass* is one traversal over a particular point: forward is pass 1, coming back over the same spot is pass 2. The two steel drums of a double-drum roller do not count as two passes.
- Passes at one location are different from the side-by-side paths needed to cover the width of the pavement. Example: a 12-ft lane with a drum of about 6.5 ft may need two paths; a 16-ft mat may need three. Four passes per path on a 16-ft mat means at least 12 passes along its length. ("Coverage lane" is the technician's own working term for a side-by-side path.)
- The paver keeps moving while the roller makes passes. A wide mat may outpace one roller, so operations may use a breakdown roller and a finishing roller. The actual count depends on drum width, overlap, pattern and coverage.
- Field observations: on a thin lift (such as one inch), vibration is generally avoided because of the risk of crushing aggregate. Some polymer mixes such as SMA may call for two early vibration passes depending on conditions. Many patterns aim to finish in about four passes plus any line passes the operator wants.

## 6. Mat temperature

Temperature behind the paver matters both for running the operation and for explaining results later.

- Take an initial reading shortly after paving begins; record the reading and where/under what conditions it was taken. Working target: 280°F or higher and under 320°F. If it is low, may tell the plant lab technician.
- Recheck after about 1,000 feet and record it. Low temperature at this checkpoint is particularly important to report.
- The technician treats about 170–176°F as the cessation temperature.
- Thin lifts cool fast; thicker lifts hold heat longer. A thick lift may keep enough heat to finish its passes starting from around 280°F, while a thin lift may cool too fast.
- Temperature records help explain a low core density later. Example: record and report a 240°F mat. It doesn't prove the cause, but it documents the conditions and that they were communicated. Document conditions outside the technician's control.

## 7. PQI setup and calibration

- The PQI measures with an electrical field. Its offset can be calibrated.
- The manufacturer's instructions recommend pre-calibrating the offset to 88% on uncompacted mat. Older screeds may read about 88% before the roller; newer ones up to about 91%. Technician experience: some screeds work better near 91%, sometimes up to 92% once dialed in. These depend on the equipment.
- If not calibrated for the current setup, calibrate. After an initial calibration the value may carry over to later work, but check it against the current setup. Start with 88% unless the paver model/age or past experience suggests otherwise, and expect to adjust.
- Calibration can happen before leaving or at the site.
- *Possible fields*: PQI ID, paver/screed info, initial and adjusted offset, reason for the change, calibration/verification status, observations.
- The PQI's measuring depth matters relative to the lift thickness.

## 8. Roller and compaction window

After the first temperature readings, tell the roller operator whether the mat is at the expected temperature, hotter or cooler. A skilled operator may change how far behind the paver they work. Work out the approximate distance (the window) the roller can work behind the paver before the mat falls too low to compact: consider current temperature, cooling rate, lift thickness, and paver and roller positions. Direct reminders may be needed.

*Possible app use*: record temperature, time and working limit, or give reminders; the technician's judgment stays in charge.

## 9. Break the mat (find the best pass count)

Used when the pass count for the material and setup isn't known or needs checking.

1. Take an initial PQI reading before the roller passes.
2. Take another reading at the same spot.
3. Roller makes a pass.
4. Read again; keep alternating pass and reading.

Readings are taken at exactly the same spot. Continue until the readings stop rising and start falling. Example: 98%, 99%, 100%, 99% — the peak is 100% and the drop after it shows the peak has passed. Record every reading with its pass number, the maximum reading, and the pass count at the maximum; use these to recommend a pass count.

Even if the calibration is off and the absolute numbers look implausible (say 98, 99, 98), the progression still shows a relative peak.

*Possible fields*: whether done, location/section, initial and successive readings, pass numbers, maximum, pass count at maximum, recommendation, calibration notes.

## 10. The core cycle

Taking random cores is the main job. Repeat this for each location the inspector gives.

**Find the location.** The state inspector gives a random location, generally after paving has passed it, so the roller operator usually can't be told in advance to work that exact spot. A location is a station plus an offset; the inspector may already have marked it. Use the measuring wheel and station information. Example: requested 135+56 — go to 136+00 and measure back 44 feet, mark with paint. Measure the offset across the pavement from the centerline or edge, consistently (the technician prefers the centerline). Keep station and offset with the core record.

**Wait for the mat to cool.** Field guidance: about 180°F is the approximate upper limit for walking without leaving footprints; wait until about 160°F. Below about 150°F it is generally possible to drive on the mat. The cessation temperature is also referenced around 176°F. Avoid footprints and tire marks; project and safety procedures govern.

**PQI reading at the location.** Position the PQI at the requested spot and read. The core is expected within about six inches of the exact spot; the technician may adjust within the allowed area to find the highest reading while staying in tolerance. Circle the exact PQI spot with the lumber crayon to preserve where it was measured and help line up the core. The PQI has a mode that takes five readings and averages them; use it and record the average. Record: percent density, MTD value, the five-reading average, station and offset, and which instrument.

MTD relates to Gmb × 62.2; typical values run about 140–150. To estimate the Gmb an MTD reading represents, divide by 62.2. Example: 144.5 ÷ 62.2 ≈ 2.323. Keep the MTD reading itself in the record, not only the converted Gmb.

**Ice the location.** After the PQI reading and the decision to core, apply ice. Rule of thumb: about 10 minutes per inch of pavement thickness; usually about half an insulated barrel, depending on the ice and the area. While icing, start the red book entries, paperwork, and prepare to core. Before moving the truck and trailer onto the spot, check temperature: prefer about 150°F or lower. Hotter may be possible but risks tire marks, especially above 160°F or near the cessation temperature (~176°F); wait longer when practical.

**Cut and remove the core.**
1. Position the truck/trailer and coring rig over the location.
2. Turn the water on before running the rig.
3. Start the generator and align the rig.
4. Know the expected pavement thickness if available. Lumber-crayon depth marks on the rig show the target depth.
5. Begin coring. Watch for air bubbles appearing or changing and for a color change in the material the water and bit are grinding out (field observations; the actual depth follows the sampling procedure).
6. Remove the rig, move the truck/trailer out of the way, secure the equipment, and make sure no tools are left in the roadway.
7. Pry the core out with the core-removal tool; it takes skill to avoid damaging it.
8. Measure the core and record its thickness. Mark the core number on the core with the lumber crayon so it can't be confused with others. Hand it to the state worker right away or later, depending on circumstances.

**Fill the core hole.** While paving is active and asphalt is available: take the shovel to the paver, scoop up about enough asphalt to fill the hole, carry it straight back, and fill the hole. If paving stops before the hole can be filled (common near the end of a shift), asphalt isn't available then. In that case remember to bring asphalt when you return the next day and fill the previous day's hole. The hole should sit in a blocked-off section until then (the technician hopes for this; it isn't guaranteed). *App*: keep an unfilled core hole as an outstanding follow-up tied to its jobsite and location, and never assume a hole was filled just because the core was recorded as removed.

## 11. During the day

**Track paving stops, skipped sections and direction changes.** As paving progresses, record the station where it stops. If a section is skipped (for example a concrete bridge that won't be paved) record both the stop station and the station where paving resumes beyond it. If paving turns around and goes the opposite way, record where it stopped and where it starts in the opposite direction. There may not be time to capture every event while work is moving; record what circumstances allow.

**Weather.** Midday is a good time to get the day's weather: the day's temperature and some wind information. Don't leave it too late — on night shifts the calendar day can change and past weather gets harder to find. Weather belongs with the day's jobsite record as context for paving and core results.

**More cores.** After the first core, repeat the cycle for each new location. Three or four cores in a day is readily possible. A very large number of cores happens with a test section, which is a separate, more involved situation not described yet. For each core keep its own identifier with its location, PQI reading, thickness, field notes and who it was handed to; never mix up records or lab results between cores.

## 12. Return to the plant and process cores

Not every core brought in is a state-requested random core:
- A **quality-control core** is one the technician/company collects for its own QC. This is different from a **state-requested core**, which is collected for the state's testing. The state may sometimes ask the technician to bring a core in for them, which the technician can do.
- The steps below were described using a QC core as the example. Apply them to other cores when the lab's process calls for the same preparation; not every state core is processed by the technician.

**Prepare a core for density testing.**
1. Open/retrieve the core.
2. Measure it.
3. Cut it, usually with a water-fed saw.
4. Saw off the bottom layer or attached material that doesn't belong to the lift being evaluated. The goal is a reading representative of the asphalt actually placed, not including underlying material that came up attached.
5. Dry the core before density testing: towel off excess water, set it in front of a fan if that's what's available, or put it in a core dryer when available.
6. If the core dryer isn't already running, turn it on and let it come up to operating condition; the core may have to wait for it.

**Use the drying time to unload.** While the core dries, unload the truck and trailer, including the PQI and other equipment carried for the job. This overlaps with drying; it doesn't have to wait until the core is fully dry.

**Then the roadway density test.** After drying and preparation, go on to the roadway density testing section of the lab workflow already in the app. That procedure lives there and isn't repeated here.

## 13. End of day

**Core handoff and paperwork.** The state-side person receiving a core usually has a sheet to fill out. The expectation is that they take the core and its paperwork to the lab when they receive it, not hold cores until the end of the day. In practice that doesn't always happen — keep track of whether handoff and delivery were prompt or delayed.

**Red book.** By the end of the day the red book should hold the essentials for each core, preserving the location, values, physical characteristics and field conditions that may explain an unusual result.

For each core:
- core number/identifier
- location: station and offset
- MTD density reading
- measured core thickness
- notes on unusual conditions or events that could have affected the pavement or the result

Everything stays under the correct core number so it can be compared to the correct lab result.

**Unusual conditions.** Record events that could help explain an unusually high, low or questionable core result. Examples: the paving crew ran out of pavement; the paver broke down; the paver sat stationary directly over the eventual core location for about 20 minutes; other delays or interruptions at or near the core. Describe what actually happened, where, and for how long if known. Don't label a core "bad" or assert a cause unless it's established; record observed facts so they can be weighed when the lab result is interpreted. This matters most for low or unexpected density results, because it preserves what was seen at the time instead of relying on memory.

**Lab results and the PQI comparison.** When the lab density results are available, record them in the red book and work out the difference between the PQI reading at each core location and the lab result for that same core. This shows how closely the PQI tracked the lab, and the technician may use the difference to adjust the PQI offset so later readings line up better. Keep each comparison tied to the matching core; never substitute a PQI reading from another location or core.

**Hand off and close.** After testing is done, results may need to be passed to the other lab technician, depending on the day. Then work back through the opening checklist in reverse: shut down and turn off what needs turning off, lock and secure the facility. This is generally the last task of the day.

## Reference

- **MTD → Gmb:** Gmb ≈ MTD ÷ 62.2 (example: 144.5 ÷ 62.2 ≈ 2.323). Typical MTD about 140–150.
- **Station back-measure:** requested 135+56 → go to 136+00 and measure back 44 ft.
- **Temperatures (technician's working numbers):** mat target 280°F to under 320°F; cessation about 170–176°F; walkable at about 180°F or less with footprint risk, wait to about 160°F; drivable below about 150°F; truck/trailer onto a core spot at about 150°F or lower.
- **Ice:** about 10 minutes per inch of pavement thickness; about half an insulated barrel.
- **Paving rate:** about 110 pounds per inch of compacted asphalt.
- **PQI offset starting point:** 88% (manufacturer), 91–92% on some screeds.

## For the implementation context (not from the technician)

Notes from reviewing the code, so the next context doesn't have to rediscover them.

- The repo's data tree is project → mix → plant → sample, with cores under plant. There is no date or shift level yet, and the diary and red book are organised by day/shift.
- The company's Roadway Daily Diary is meant as a *lens* over the recorded data: the app produces text to copy into the diary, with no double entry.
- Follow the project rules: one navigation built from the one data object; framework files (`qc-*.js`) stay free of asphalt knowledge, module data lives under `modules/asphalt/`; reuse existing helpers (`createTestRecord`, `hasNumber`, `commitChange`, `buildRow`, `buildSourceView`) instead of writing new ones.
- A new record type should only need a test definition in the module; the record shape, navigation entry and screens come from it.
